Biggest-ever map of brain region crucial to movement shines light on conditions including Parkinson’s, schizophrenia and autism

![A reconstruction of an adult human brain atlas, with various anatomical regions highlighted in different colours](https://images.ft.com/v3/image/raw/ftcms%3A11aaf9b5-600c-481c-a8e3-c3d4e160c7d4?source=next-article&fit=scale-down&quality=highest&width=1440&dpr=1)

A reconstruction of an adult human brain with anatomical regions highlighted, viewed from underneath © Allen Institute/Song-Lin Ding


Scientists have built the biggest-ever multi-species atlas of a brain region crucial to movement, emotions and decision-making, paving the way to potential breakthroughs for conditions including Parkinson’s disease, bipolar disorder and autism.

Researchers found a new type of cell tied to schizophrenia, a potential gene therapy to reduce Parkinson’s tremors and changes in gene expression linked to ageing.

The effort, involving hundreds of scientists from more than 50 institutions across nine countries, highlights how a combination of technological advances and international collaboration is opening new possibilities to combat destructive neurological problems.

The basal ganglia are the “grand central” for many brain diseases, said Evan Macosko, a Harvard Medical School associate professor who worked on the research.

“We really need to have a clear understanding of how these different brain areas function if we’re going to try and fix them,” said Macosko, a core member of the Broad Institute for biomedical research. “Our hope is that this basic map that we’ve laid out will be that template that many neuroscientists can build on to help them understand this complex and important structure.”

The atlas uses data from humans, macaques, marmosets and mice, according to more than two dozen papers on the work published in the Cell Press journal family on Thursday. The researchers profiled 25.5mn cells, most of them from people, using state of the art analysis of the activity of genes in them.

“Disease conditions are always cell type-specific, so in order to understand what’s really going on in the brain, you have to have an accurate cell type list and examine individual gene expression in each cell type,” said Hongkui Zeng, executive vice-president and director of brain science at the Allen Institute, which was also involved in the research.

The scientists discovered how a rare type of nerve cell was linked to raised schizophrenia risk. This will allow researchers to focus future work on people who have the same genetic disease signal.

A second study found that an amended version of an existing gene therapy for Parkinson’s was more effective at reducing tremors in animals, as well as being less invasive.

### Basal ganglia

A critical brain region implicated in movement, psychiatric and neuro-developmental disorders

A further research strand uncovered evidence that genetic changes in certain basal ganglia cell types could play a role in autism. Another group of scientists showed how ageing could be predicted from gene activity patterns across multiple cell types.

The research provided an “essential roadmap” to a brain region without which “we cannot breathe, we cannot move, we can’t live”, said Frank Hirth, professor of evolutionary neuroscience at King’s College London. “This complex neural network co-ordinates involuntary and voluntary motor actions, motivated behaviours and decision-making, and is required for internal representations of embodiment and the sensory surround,” Hirth said. “You close your eyes and can’t see, but you know where the door is to walk out of the room. You think of grandma’s heavenly crumble and want to call to say I miss you.”

Other scientists not involved in the work welcomed its scale and scope, pointing to ways it could be further developed. It would be crucial to see how the “molecular snapshots” corresponded to the behaviour of living cells, said Nina Rzechorzek, an MRC clinician scientist at the University of Cambridge.

“The next step is to establish which molecular differences contribute causally to dysfunction, which reflect potentially reversible adaptations, and whether intervening in those processes improves brain health,” Rzechorzek said. “This requires functional experiments and independent mechanistic validation alongside increasingly sophisticated molecular reference maps.”
